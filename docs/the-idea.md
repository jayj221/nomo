# NOMO — The Idea, in Full

> A concept memo. Use it to pitch, to brief a collaborator, or as the backbone of an application essay. It explains not just *what* Nomo is, but *why every part of it exists.*

---

## The one line

**Nomo gives you ten guaranteed matches a day, in your league, whose faces stay blurred until you actually talk — so you fall for who someone is before you judge how they look.**

## The elevator version

Every dating app today runs on the same engine: a face, judged in a tenth of a second, and an endless feed you swipe until you give up. It's designed to keep you *on the app*, not to get you *off it*. Nomo inverts the whole thing. Each morning you get exactly ten people, picked by AI to be on your level and ranked by how much of your world they share. You see their name, age, and a few one-word hobbies — but their photo is blurred. You get two reveals a day, so you can't just unblur everyone; you have to be curious about the person first. Once a day, a one-hour window opens for everyone at once, and the people you're both interested in become a live voice conversation. Talk first. Faces come later. It's the opposite of a slot machine — it's a daily ritual built to actually end in a real connection.

---

## 1. The problem (and why it's a *mechanics* problem, not a taste problem)

People don't hate dating apps because they're ugly. They hate them because of how they're *built*:

- **The face-first filter.** A photo grid forces a snap judgment before a single word is exchanged. Research and lived experience both say the same thing: we decide "yes/no" on a face in milliseconds, and that judgment is shallow, biased, and a bad predictor of whether two people will actually click.
- **Infinite supply kills intention.** When there are a thousand more profiles behind this one, no single person matters. Abundance makes everyone disposable. You swipe fast, match carelessly, and ghost easily.
- **The business model is the enemy of the mission.** Swipe apps make money when you *stay single and keep scrolling*. Their incentive is your loneliness. A product that got you into a great relationship in a week would be a commercial failure for them.
- **Matches that never say a word.** Most matches die in the inbox. The "hey" that goes nowhere is the defining experience of modern dating.

So the problem isn't "make a prettier dating app." It's: **the core mechanic is wrong.** Fix the mechanic and everything downstream changes.

## 2. The insight

Three beliefs sit under Nomo:

1. **A voice tells you more in ten minutes than a face tells you in a tenth of a second.** Tone, humour, how someone listens, whether they're kind — none of that survives a photo grid, all of it survives a conversation. So make the conversation come first.
2. **Scarcity creates intention.** If you only get ten people, and only two reveals, and one hour a day, each of those becomes *precious*. You show up. You pay attention. You choose on purpose. Abundance is the disease; deliberate scarcity is the cure.
3. **The best matching signal isn't looks — it's overlap of world.** Music, mentality, the 2am version of a person. Rank on *that*, and keep looks as a slow, earned reveal rather than the opening filter.

## 3. How Nomo works (the full journey)

**Onboarding.** You upload a few photos. On your device, an AI places you in an attractiveness *bracket* (1–10) — you see this number exactly once, and then never again, and no one else ever sees it. It's used only so your daily matches are roughly in your league, which quietly removes the mismatch and rejection that makes dating apps brutal. You write two or three prompt answers (this is your voice, in words). You pick vibe tags — music and mentality. You say who you want to meet.

**Your daily ten.** Every morning, ten people appear — all in your bracket, ranked by how much of your world they share. Each one shows a **blurred photo, their name, their age, and a few one-word hobbies underneath.** You can tap any of them to read their prompts — the real, written, human part. What you *can't* do is see their face freely.

**Two reveals a day.** You get to un-blur exactly two photos per day. That's the whole tension of the product: you have to decide *who's worth a reveal* based on their words and vibe, not the other way around. It makes looking someone in the eye feel like a small, earned event again.

**Marking who you'd talk to.** You mark the people you'd actually want to speak with. Nothing happens immediately — Nomo isn't instant-gratification.

**The window.** Once a day, at a moment nobody knows in advance, everyone's phone lights up at the same time (a shared, BeReal-style ritual). For one hour, the window is open. The people you *both* marked are now available for a **live voice call** — or a text conversation, if that's what you both prefer. You start with your number one; if the vibe isn't there, you move down your list.

**Talking, then seeing.** On the call you're still anonymous — two voices. If it's going well, either of you can ask to see the other; it only happens if you *both* ask. Then the photo un-blurs, together, at the same moment. Names and the rest unlock the same mutual way.

**Keeping each other.** If the hour ends and you both want more, you become a real match — a standing conversation you can return to every day, with the option to trade socials on your own terms. If it wasn't right, no harm: tomorrow brings ten more.

## 4. Why each rule exists (the design is the argument)

| Rule | Why it's there |
|---|---|
| **Exactly ten a day** | Enough choice to matter, few enough to take each person seriously. No infinite feed, so no disposability. |
| **Guaranteed** | You never open the app to nothing. Every day has ten real people — the opposite of the empty-inbox death of other apps. |
| **Blurred photos + name/age/hobbies** | You judge the person before the face. But it's not fully blind — name, age and hobbies give you enough to be *curious*, not enough to be shallow. |
| **Two reveals a day** | Makes seeing someone's face an intentional act with a cost, not a reflex. Rations the shallow signal. |
| **Bracket matching (private, once)** | Removes painful mismatch and rejection, without ever turning attractiveness into a public score. |
| **One shared window, one hour** | A ritual, not a habit-loop. Everyone shows up at once, which solves the "no one's online when I am" problem and makes the moment feel alive. |
| **Voice first, text allowed** | Voice is the richest cheap signal for chemistry. But forcing it would exclude people; text is a graceful fallback. |
| **Mutual, progressive reveal** | Nobody is exposed one-sidedly. Consent is built into the mechanic, not bolted on. |
| **You keep each other after** | The app's success is a relationship that leaves the app — the exact opposite of the swipe-app incentive. |

## 5. What makes it different

- **vs Tinder** — Tinder is face-first, infinite, fast. Nomo is words-first, finite, slow.
- **vs Hinge** — Hinge added prompts but still leads with photos and infinite likes. Nomo blurs the photo and rations everything.
- **vs BeReal** — BeReal proved the "everyone at once, once a day" ritual works. Nomo borrows that energy for dating, which no one has done well.
- **vs voice/blind-dating gimmicks** — those tend to be one-off stunts. Nomo builds the blindness into a repeatable daily system with a real matching engine underneath.

The defensibility isn't the idea (ideas are cheap, and others are circling this space). It's execution, the ritual habit, the density of a real community, and the brand — a product that credibly stands for "no more shallow, no more nowhere."

## 6. The name

**Nomo** = "no more." No more faces you judge in a tenth of a second. No more matches that never say a word. No more talk that goes nowhere. No more apps designed to keep you single. And the closing line the whole brand builds to: **"Single? Nomo."** The logo — two overlapping rings, two people meeting in the middle — is the mark of that.

## 7. Who it's for

Young adults (roughly 20–30 to start) who are tired of swipe fatigue and actually want something real — people for whom the *ritual* and *intentionality* are the draw, not endless options. Launch narrow: one city or one campus, win it completely (density is everything in dating — ten matches spread across the world is dead; ten in your own city is magic), then expand.

## 8. Why now

- Swipe fatigue and "dating app burnout" are mainstream, named, and complained about loudly.
- BeReal made the "synchronous daily ritual" a proven, understood format.
- On-device AI makes private, phone-side analysis (for bracketing) feasible without shipping faces to a server.
- A cultural turn toward authenticity and against dopamine-engineered feeds.

## 9. The honest tensions (name them; don't hide them)

A mature version of this idea is upfront about its hard parts:

- **AI attractiveness bracketing is ethically loaded.** Ranking people by looks, even privately, even only to route matches, is exactly the kind of thing that can be biased, hurtful, or a reputational grenade if mishandled or leaked. It has to stay private, framed as *placement* not *grade*, and it deserves a serious "should this exist at all, or is vibe-only matching better?" conversation. This tension is not a footnote — grappling with it openly is part of building the thing responsibly.
- **Faces and voices are biometric data.** The moment you analyse them, real laws apply (Illinois BIPA, GDPR, India's DPDP Act). Consent, retention limits, and legal review are non-negotiable before real users. The safest v1 may launch *without* the scoring and biometrics and prove the core loop first.
- **Safety is existential for a dating product.** Blocking, reporting, and a real response to abuse aren't features — they're the license to operate.

## 10. How it eventually makes money

Not by keeping you single. When people love it, a light subscription for *more of the good thing*: extra reveals, an extra window, seeing who marked you. Never pay-to-be-seen, never pay-to-win-attention — that would rot the trust the whole product is built on. The north-star metric is not time-in-app; it's **connections that turn into real conversations that continue.**

## 11. The bigger vision

Start as the anti-dating-app: the place where you talk first and mean it. If it works, Nomo becomes a broader statement about how technology should introduce people — with intention, consent, and patience instead of infinite, shallow, engineered choice. The measure of success is the number of people who *leave* because they found someone. An app that's proud to lose you to a relationship.
